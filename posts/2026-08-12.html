Prime Minister Launches Cross-Party Social Care Reform Initiative as UK Elderly Care System Faces Mounting Pressure

<cite index="60-1,60-8">Prime Minister Andy Burnham launches cross-party talks on social care reform, bringing together opposition leaders to develop urgent reforms for England's adult social care system.</cite>

Something big shifted in the care world recently. On 29 July 2026, UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham stood up and said what millions of families already know: the social care system needs to change. Across Europe, governments are facing the same pressure — populations are ageing, and the systems built decades ago can't keep up. The EU's working-age population is shrinking in 22 out of 27 member states by 2050, according to Bruegel think tank analysis. For anyone caring for a parent, grandparent, or loved one, this isn't just news — it's your life right now.

In England, Prime Minister Burnham announced a major overhaul. A&E attendances by people over 65 have climbed from 3.3 million in 2011/12 to 5.8 million in 2024/25, according to NHS data — a sign that social care funding hasn't kept pace with need. Burnham's plan focuses on the care workforce: better pay, proper career paths, job security, and stronger links with the NHS. Baroness Louise Casey's Independent Commission on Adult Social Care will now deliver its final report by summer 2027 instead of 2028, with cross-party talks already underway. A new Dementia Tsar is expected before the end of summer 2026.

Ireland faces the same urgency. By 2057, one in three people will be aged 60 or older, and close to 700,000 will be aged 80 or over — many needing publicly funded care, according to Ireland's Commission on Care for Older People. The problems are already hitting hard: Ireland has one of the lowest rates of long-term care workers in the EU, home support is uneven (especially in rural areas), and there's still no statutory home care scheme. Active Retirement Ireland has pushed for an independent Commissioner for Ageing and Older People, and automatic medical card access for anyone over 80.

Funding the shift will be steep. The Health Foundation estimates that introducing a Scottish-style model of free personal care in England would cost around £6 billion in 2026/27, rising to £7 billion by 2035/36. Meanwhile, 2 million people aged 65 and over and 1.5 million unpaid carers are already missing out on care they need, according to the parliamentary Health and Social Care Committee. Care workers are twice as likely to be claiming benefits as workers in other sectors.

For families and carers, the next few years will test whether politicians follow through. Both England and Ireland have reform commissions now in motion, with named leaders and cross-party backing — that's rare in care policy. But as carers know, roadmaps aren't enough. The families around kitchen tables working out how to look after a parent deserve a system that actually works, not just a promise that it might one day.
