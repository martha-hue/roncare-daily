CIOs Discover New Frontline Challenge: Ensuring AI Accuracy and Accountability as Systems Embed Across Healthcare

Health systems pivot from AI deployment to governance, finding that success depends less on new models than on managing existing ones responsibly.

Something significant is happening in hospitals and clinics right now, and if you've ever sat on hold trying to book a referral, or watched a nurse scribble notes instead of talking to you, you'll care about it. AI has quietly moved from the lab into the everyday machinery of healthcare — scheduling, paperwork, insurance approvals, clinical notes — and the pace is accelerating fast.

One of the biggest practical changes hitting hospitals right now is AI stepping into the paperwork nightmare known as prior authorisation — the process where doctors must get insurer approval before treating you. According to the American Medical Association, 93% of physicians say these delays hurt patient care, and 29% have witnessed a serious adverse event because a treatment was held up waiting for approval. AI systems are now handling the entire submission process: reading patient records, matching them against insurer rules, drafting the approval request, and tracking the response — all without a coordinator having to touch it.

Microsoft made a major move that's worth knowing about if you have a loved one seeing multiple specialists. The company unveiled upgrades to Dragon Copilot, its clinical AI assistant, which bridges the gap between a patient's medical records and the operational side of a doctor's day — emails, schedules, hospital policy documents — all in one place. A clinician can pull up your lab results, check the relevant treatment guidelines, and review their own calendar without toggling between different systems. Emergency department doctors at one health system cut their documentation time by 42% in early pilots.

The financial stakes are becoming clearer. Nurses currently spend 15 to 20 minutes of every hour on administrative tasks — time that could go to patients. The American Hospital Association points to automation as a genuine tool for freeing up that time, especially as health systems move beyond small pilots into full workflow redesigns. At the same time, a report from the Peterson Health Technology Institute flagged a real concern: AI can also increase 'billing intensity,' meaning it sometimes helps organisations document more aggressively to boost revenue, which inflates costs across the system. The good outcomes are real, but so are the risks if AI is pointed in the wrong direction.

What all of this adds up to is a healthcare system in the middle of a genuine shift — not a future one, a present one. The tools are here, the early results are promising, and the regulatory guardrails are starting to catch up. For anyone navigating the system as a patient or a carer, the near-term payoff should be shorter waits, fewer lost referrals, and doctors who have more time to actually look at you rather than a screen. The longer-term work is making sure those benefits reach everyone — including the rural hospitals and smaller clinics that have historically been left behind.
