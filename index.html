Europe's Long-Term Care System Faces Structural Failure as Governments Treat Elderly Care as Secondary Priority

Analysis reveals that European countries' policy choices, not demographics alone, are driving systemic neglect in elder care provision.

Europe is ageing faster than at any point in modern history, and the pressure on health and care systems is becoming impossible to ignore. The EU's share of people aged 85 and over is set to more than double by 2050. For families watching a parent struggle at home, or a carer working a 12-hour shift for barely above minimum wage, this is not a distant policy problem — it is already their daily reality.

The UK has signalled real political momentum on this issue. In July 2026, Prime Minister Andy Burnham described the social care system as broken and vowed to end decades of political drift. Baroness Louise Casey's Independent Commission on Adult Social Care was asked to deliver final recommendations by summer 2027 — a year ahead of schedule. The 'Big Conversation on Care' launched alongside this, aiming to gather views from the public on who should receive care and who should pay for it. Burnham also invited opposition leaders to cross-party talks, acknowledging that 22 previous attempts to reform social care have failed.

The numbers show why urgency matters. A&E attendances by people over 65 in England rose from 3.3 million in 2011/12 to 5.8 million in 2024/25, driven partly by lack of social care support. England's social care workforce has around 111,000 unfilled posts, with a vacancy rate of 6.8% in 2024–25. Two in three care workers cite impact on their health and wellbeing as a reason for wanting to leave. The Health Foundation estimates that a Scottish-style model of free personal care for over-65s in England would cost around £6.5 billion extra in 2026/27, rising to £7.5 billion by 2035/36.

Ireland faces similar pressures. Social Justice Ireland warned in its Budget 2027 submission that over 122,000 older people were living in poverty in 2025, up 15% on the year before. Age Action Ireland found more than 6,000 people on waiting lists for home care packages, with many waiting six months or longer. Ireland's Programme for Government commits to a statutory home care scheme that would give people a legal right to care at home, but Home and Community Care Ireland says progress has been slow since development began in 2016. Government spending on home care rose from €487 million in 2018 to €723 million in 2023, yet advocates say it still falls short as Ireland's old-age dependency ratio is projected to double by 2052.

For families, carers, and older people themselves, the picture is both hopeful and sobering. There is real political momentum in the UK that has not existed for a generation. But the gap between what is promised and what is funded remains enormous, and real people are waiting on real lists right now. The decisions made in the next 12 to 18 months will shape whether ageing well becomes a genuine expectation or a privilege only some can afford.
